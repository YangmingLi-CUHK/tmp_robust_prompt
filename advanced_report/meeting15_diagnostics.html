5.2 Why This Happens

GCL Linear Probe training: Linear(embedding) → class.
The linear classifier has only 1799 parameters (256×7+7). It is trained on 35 labeled node embeddings produced by the frozen GNN. The training signal is: "given this frozen 256-dim vector, predict the class." No graph edges are involved in the classifier's learning. The graph is only used during the GNN forward pass to produce embeddings — and the GNN was pretrained on the clean graph via GraphCL, so its aggregation weights encode clean-graph structural patterns.
GPPT / RobustPrompt-T training: prompt(GNN(prompted_x, attacked_edge_index), attacked_edge_index) → class.
GPPT learns prompt tokens by computing similarity between node embeddings along graph edges. RobustPrompt-T adds defense prompts selected by graph-structural heuristics and prunes edges via τ_tune. Both methods use graph edges as part of their learning signal. At ptb ≥ 0.20, 25–31% of these edges are attacker-controlled. The model fits the noise.
The paradox resolved: At high ptb, any method that learns from graph structure during downstream training is worse than a method that ignores it.

This is a fundamental limitation of the Transductive Few-Shot Prompt Learning paradigm under structural attack: the graph is the only source of additional information beyond the 35 labels, but when the graph is heavily corrupted, it becomes a liability rather than an asset. The linear probe's "ignorance" of structure becomes a feature, not a bug.

Implication: For ptb ≥ 0.20 on Cora with 5-shot, no graph-based prompt method can be expected to beat a simple linear probe. The attack has destroyed too much of the structural signal that prompt tuning relies on. The defense problem shifts from "how to identify and remove attack edges" to "whether to use the graph at all."
